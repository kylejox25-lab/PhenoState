single-cell perturbation response prediction

Single-cell perturbation technologies enable systematic investigation 
of gene functions and regulatory networks with single-cell resolution. 
However, performing large-scale and combinatorial perturbation screens 
poses notable challenges due to their exponentially increased complexity. 
Computational methods, including foundation models, have been 
developed to predict perturbation effects. Yet despite claims of promising 
performance, concerns remain about their true efficacy, particularly when 
evaluated across diverse and previously unseen cellular contexts and 
perturbation scenarios. Here, we present a comprehensive benchmark of 
27 methods for single-cell perturbation response prediction, evaluated 
across 29 datasets using 6 complementary performance metrics. By 
evaluating them under multiple scenarios, we systematically assess their 
generalizability, including that of emerging foundation models. Our results 
provide practical guidance for method selection and underscore the need 
for cellular context embedding approaches to enhance the generalizability 
of perturbation effect prediction in single-cell research.
Single-cell perturbation technology (for example, Perturb-seq and 
sciPlex) provides an innovative platform for integrating single-cell 
sequencing technologies with targeted perturbations, such as gene 
knockouts, knockdowns and chemical treatments, to reveal gene func-
tions, regulatory networks and mechanisms driving cell states at a 
single-cell resolution1– 3. Despite its immense potential, the application 
of single-cell perturbation sequencing at large scales faces substantial 
practical and economic barriers. Conducting large-scale perturbation 
screens, particularly for combined perturbations, is infeasible because 
of the exponential growth in complexity.
T o address the challenges associated with large-scale perturbation 
screens, various computational methods have been developed to pre-
dict perturbation effects. These computational methods can generally 
be classified into two major categories on the basis of their application 
scenarios. The first category, defined as the ‘cellular context generali-
zation scenario’ , involves training a model on data measured in a set

Results
A comprehensive framework for benchmarking single-cell 
perturbation effect prediction
Methods. T o thoroughly assess the performance of prediction meth-
ods in this field, we developed a comprehensive benchmark framework, 
including state-of-the-art methods, diverse datasets and rigorous 
evaluation metrics (Fig. 1a). In this study, we evaluated the methods 
across two key scenarios: cellular context generalization and pertur-
bation generalization (Fig. 1a). While we aimed to include a diverse set 
of methods in our evaluation, several tools were excluded for specific 
reasons (Supplementary Note 1). As a result, in the cellular context 
generalization scenario, we examined 10 methods from published 
studies: biolord16, CellOT14, inVAE17, scDisInFact18, scGen4, scPRAM15, 
scPreGAN19, SCREEN20, scVIDR21 and trVAE22. In the perturbation gener-
alization scenario, we examined another 14 methods: AttentionPert23, 
biolord16, CPA24, GEARS5, GenePert25, linearModel12, scFoundation6, 
scGPT7, chemCPA26, scouter27, scELMo28, GeneCompass29, PRnet30 and 
cycleCDR31. Given the growing concerns raised in recent literature 
regarding the effectiveness of advanced machine learning models, 
particularly foundation models, we also included 4 baseline models 
named baseReg, baseMLP, baseControl and trainMean (Methods). 
These baseline models serve as benchmarks to determine whether 
more complex methods learn additional meaningful information 
from the data. In total, our evaluation includes 23 published methods 
and 4 baseline models, covering a broad spectrum of methodological 
approaches in single-cell perturbation effect prediction.
Datasets. The systematic benchmark relies on a collection of diverse 
datasets designed to reflect real-world biological challenges. For 
the cellular context generalization scenario, we curated 12 datasets 
categorized on the basis of the cellular context 1,32– 37 (Fig. 1b). These 
datasets include 8 that are focused on cross-cell-line predictions  
(predicting perturbation effects in a new cell type), 3 that are focused 
on cross-patient predictions (predicting perturbation effects in  
a new patient) and one that is focused on cross-species predictions  
(predicting perturbation effects in a new species). Each dataset  
challenges the ability of the models to generalize perturbation  
effects across contexts. Additionally, the datasets can be further 
gr
ouped on the basis of the presence of perturbation metadata.  
Of the 12 datasets, 7 contain only perturbation data (single-condition 
datasets), and the remaining 5 include additional perturbation  
metadata, that is, time-point/dosage information (multi-condition 
datasets).

then using the model to predict the effects of that perturbation in a dif-
ferent, unobserved cellular context. A prominent tool in this category 
is scGen, which is based on variational autoencoders and enables pre-
dictions to be generated across diverse cellular contexts4. The second 
category, defined as the ‘perturbation generalization scenario’ , involves  
training a model on the effects of a set of perturbations, which are 
usually measured in a specific cellular context, and then using the 
model to predict the effects of unseen perturbations. Notable methods  
in this category include GEARS, which is based on knowledge graphs 
of gene–gene relationships that can be generalized to unseen 
perturbed genes5.
In recent years, numerous methods have been presented in  
the field of single-cell perturbation effect prediction for both of 
the aforementioned scenarios. In addition, single-cell foundation  
models have established a paradigm for in silico perturbation predic-
tion through pretraining and fine-tuning strategies6– 9. Models such as 
scGPT and scFoundation, which utilize large-scale pretrained archi -
tectures, have demonstrated the potential to predict perturbation 
effects across diverse cellular and perturbation contexts6,7. However, 
recent studies have continued to raise concerns regarding the true 
effectiveness of these models10– 12. Specifically, many of these methods 
do not outperform baseline models or linear models on the basis of 
straightforward assumptions when they are evaluated across multiple 
datasets. This has led to a growing call for more comprehensive and 
rigorous assessments of these methods. T o address this, researchers 
should carefully examine several key aspects of single-cell perturba-
tion effective modeling methods, including the comprehensiveness 
of the test scenarios they are designed for, the diversity of the datasets 
used and the thoroughness of the evaluation metrics. Only by properly 
and thoroughly addressing these factors can we obtain a reliable and 
clear understanding of the strengths and limitations of these methods. 
Furthermore, it is crucial for an evaluation framework to go beyond 
performance tests and present applicable guidance and solutions 
for the development of more reliable and generalizable models in 
this domain.
In this study, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of single-cell 
perturbation prediction methods, where the main procedure is as fol-
lows: (1) In the cellular context generalization scenario, we compare 14 
methods across 12 datasets that cover a wide range of cellular contexts 
from different cell lines, participant samples, and even different spe-
cies to investigate the generalizability of the methods across different 
cellular contexts; (2) in the perturbation generalization scenario, we 
compare 18 methods across 17 datasets to investigate generalizability 
to unseen perturbations; (3) in both scenarios, we use 6 evaluation 
metrics to comprehensively assess prediction accuracy, including 
the mean squared error (MSE), Pearson correlation coefficient delta 
(PCC-delta) 5, E-distance 13, Wasserstein distance 14, Kullback–Leibler 
divergence (KL-divergence) and common differentially expressed 
genes (Common-DEGs) 15; and (4) we utilize simulated data in both 
scenarios to assess the scalability of the evaluated methods and to 
investigate impacts of noise and sparsity on the performance of those 
methods. Analysis results obtained from the comprehensive bench-
mark are presented, including the following: (1) Our study indicated 
that there is no ‘one-size-fits-all’ method that works well across all 
datasets. Some limitations of current methods, including foundation 
models for both test scenarios, were identified. In the cellular context 
generalization scenario, owing to limitations in algorithm design, that 
is, neglecting the specificity of perturbation responses across differ-
ent cellular contexts (intercellular heterogeneity, or for simplicity 
inter-heterogeneity), all methods likely exhibit poor generalizability. 
Specifically, when predicting the effects of perturbations in a new 
cellular context that is quite different from the training datasets, all 
methods performed poorly, even worse than the baseline models; (2)

current tools for single-cell perturbation prediction comprehensively, 
we focused on three key metrics, that is, accuracy, robustness and 
scalability.

fair, interpretable and representative evaluation of perturbation

Benchmarking analysis for the cellular context generalization 
scenario
In the cellular context generalization scenario, we evaluated the predic-
tion of known perturbations in previously unobserved cellular contexts. 
The cellular context generalization scenario was further divided into 
two distinct test settings by partitioning the training and test datasets 
into independent and identically distributed or in-distribution (i.i.d.) 
and out-of-distribution (o.o.d.) settings (Fig. 2a)14,18. In the o.o.d. set-
ting, we used leave-one-out cross-validation, where one context was 
held out as the test set, while the remaining contexts served as the 
training set (Supplementary Note 3). In the i.i.d. scenario, the model 
was trained using the same training data as in the o.o.d. scenario but 
with access to some of the cells from the held-out context. These extra 
training cells and the test cells were assumed to be independent and 
identically distributed14. In practical applications, the o.o.d. scenario 
is more commonly encountered, as we often aim to predict the effects 
of perturbations in a new, previously unseen context. Furthermore, 
the i.i.d. setting can be considered a special case of the o.o.d. scenario, 
where the test context has a highly similar counterpart in the training 
data. Therefore, in the following benchmark analysis, we focus primar-
ily on the o.o.d. setting scenario, and the results of the i.i.d. setting 
scenario can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1.

objective is to minimize population-average metrics such as MSE, 
inVAE and trVAE are the most suitable options. If the objective is to 
minimize population-distribution metrics such as KL-divergence and 
Common-DEGs, scVIDR and scPRAM are the most suitable options. 
Furthermore, we found that, in contrast to population-average metrics, 
all methods performed relatively poorly on population-distribution 
metrics, particularly on the Common-DEGs metric. For instance, in the 
Haber dataset, although the top-performing method achieved an aver-
age PCC-delta of 0.86, uniform manifold approximation and projection 
visualization revealed a substantial discrepancy between the predicted 
and ground-truth distributions (Supplementary Fig. 12). Furthermore, 
on average, only about 10% of the true most differentially expressed 
genes were recovered. We conduct a more in-depth discussion of the 
underlying reasons for this in the next section on the ‘perturbation 
generalization scenario’ .
In addition, among the 12 benchmark datasets used in this study, 
5 included additional perturbation metadata, such as time-point/dos-
age information, and these are classified as multi-condition datasets 
(Fig. 2c). Among the 14 methods tested, only biolord, scDisInFact and 
scVIDR utilized these additional metadata 16,18,21. Through uniform 
manifold approximation and projection visualization, calculation of sil-
houette score51 and multifactor analysis of variance (Fig. 2d, Extended 
Data Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Fig. 7and8, Supplementary Notes 4 
and5 and Supplementary Table 2), time-point/dosage covariates gen-
erally had a substantial effect on the cellular response. We evaluated 
the performance of biolord and scDisInFact on these datasets, as well 
as the performance of scVIDR on the TCDD and sciPlex3 dataset, to 
assess whether these tools were optimal for use with multi-condition 
datasets. However, biolord and scDisInFact did not outperform the 
tools that did not incorporate covariates on these datasets (Fig. 2c ). 
This may be because, although the time-point/dosage covariates 
provided useful information, the baseline performance of biolord 
and scDisInFact—without incorporating covariate information—was 
relatively suboptimal. As a result, despite performance improvements 
upon the inclusion of covariates, these models did not achieve top 
rankings. T o validate this, we calculated the baseline performance 
of biolord and scDisInFact on these datasets. In the comparison, the 
performance of biolord was generally better than that of its baseline 
model (Fig. 2e). This analysis indicated that incorporating covariate 
information improved the model’s performance. However, in some 
cases, scDisInFact did not improve from its baseline value (Fig. 2f ). 
This aligns with the primary design focus of scDisInFact, which is to 
address batch effects and integrate single-cell datasets rather than to 
predict perturbation effects
18. The scVIDR method achieved the best 
overall performance on the TCDD dataset and ranked second on the 
sciPlex3 dataset. Therefore, for a dataset that includes perturbation 
dosage information, scVIDR is an optimal choice.
Next, we assessed the robustness of the perturbation effect predic-
tion algorithms to technical noise and data sparsity using simulated 
data50. As the noise and sparsity in the data increased, the perfor -
mance of all methods decreased markedly (Fig. 2g). We also noted 
that the performance of the algorithms deteriorated more severely for 
high-sparsity data, indicating that their performance is more sensitive 
to data sparsity (Fig. 2b). In terms of scalability (Supplementary Note 8), 
none of the algorithms encountered out-of-memory issues, and their 
runtimes were within acceptable limits, except for CellOT (Fig. 2h). 
The increased computational complexity of CellOT arises from its 
explicit modeling of cell-to-cell heterogeneity, and it currently does 
not support GPU acceleration.
In summary, the following insights are obtained regarding the 
benchmark analysis of the cellular context generalization scenario: 
(1) trVAE, CellOT and inVAE demonstrated the best overall perfor -
mance on single-condition datasets. For accurate prediction of DEGs, 
scPRAM is the optimal choice. Additionally, when the dataset includes 
perturbation dosage information, such as in chemical perturbation 
datasets, scVIDR is the best method to use. (2) Models that consider 
covariates affecting cellular responses provide more accurate pre -
dictions of single-cell perturbation effects. However, most existing 
tools do not incorporate covariates, and those that do are still far 
from being sufficiently accurate. Given the increasing availability 
of multi-condition data in the single-cell perturbation field, there 
is a pressing need to develop high-performing models tailored for 
multi-condition data, which can fully exploit covariate information to 
achieve improved prediction accuracy. (3) All the algorithms’ perfor-
mance was affected by data noise and sparsity, with greater sensitivity 
to sparsity. (4) In terms of scalability, none of the algorithms encoun-
tered out-of-memory issues, and their runtimes were within acceptable 
limits, except for CellOT.
Limitations of current methods in the cellular context 
generalization scenario
We observed substantial performance variability among the 14 algo-
rithms across the 12 datasets in the cellular context generalization 
test. In some datasets, all methods achieved excellent performance, 
whereas in others, the performance of all methods was notably poor. 
For example, in the Afriat dataset, even the trainMean baseline per-
formed exceptionally well, with a median PCC-delta of 0.92 and an 
average value of 0.85 (Fig. 2c). On the other hand, in datasets such 
as KaggleCrossCell, all machine learning models performed poorly, 
with a median PCC-delta of 0.54 and an average value of 0.52 (Fig. 2c).
T o investigate the underlying reasons for these discrepancies, we 
conducted the following analyses: (1) We compared the performance 
of the methods in the i.i.d. and o.o.d. scenarios. As mentioned earlier, 
in the i.i.d. scenario, the model was trained using the same training 
data as in the o.o.d. scenario but with additional access to half of the 
cells from the held-out context. In other words, in the i.i.d. scenario, 
we can assume that for each test context, the training dataset always 
includes a context that is highly similar to it. Therefore, the predic -
tion task in i.i.d. scenarios is simpler, while making predictions for 
the same cellular context in o.o.d scenarios is more challenging and 
places greater demands on the model’s generalization capabilities.

evaluated methods is highly dependent on the inter-heterogeneity.

differs substantially from the training datasets, all methods likely 
performed unsatisfactorily, highlighting their limited generalizability. 
We believe that accounting for intercellular heterogeneity is crucial for 
enhancing the generalizability of current models across diverse cellular 
contexts. Finally, user guidance is provided regarding the benchmark 
analysis of the cellular context generalization scenario (Fig. 3g).
Benchmarking analysis of the perturbation generalization 
scenario
In the perturbation generalization scenario, we assess the ability of 
models to predict the effects of previously unobserved perturbations 
within a specific cellular context. Depending on the type of pertur -
bation, this scenario can be further divided into two categories: genetic 
perturbation effect prediction and chemical perturbation effect  
prediction. (1) Genetic perturbation effect prediction. In this scenario, 
we evaluated the performance of 11 existing algorithms, including  
the foundation models—AttentionPert 23, biolord16, CPA24, GEARS5,  
GenePert25, linearModel12, scouter27, scFoundation6, scGPT7, scELMo28 
and GeneCompass29—as well as 4 baseline models, on 13 datasets of 
varying sizes, including between 25 and 1,618 perturbations. In addi-
tion, recent studies suggest that for predicting gene expression profiles 
under combined perturbations, the simplest additive model—which 
predicts the effects of combined perturbations as the sum of the effects 
of two single perturbations—often outperforms complex deep learn-
ing models or foundation models such as scGPT 12. However, these 
conclusions were derived from a single dataset. T o address this limi-
tation in the previous evaluations, we included 4 datasets focused  
on combined perturbations (two-gene combinations) in our evaluation. 
(2) Chemical perturbation effect prediction. Similarly, in chemical 
perturbation effect prediction scenarios, datasets are categorized  
into single-perturbation datasets and combined-perturbation datasets. 
Among the methods tested, biolord16, chemCPA26 and cycleCDR31 are 
limited to single-perturbation datasets.
Genetic perturbation setting.  Across the 9 single-perturbation 
datasets, GenePert, scGPT and scouter demonstrated the best 
overall performance (Fig. 4a). Both GenePert and scouter leverage 
ChatGPT-derived textual descriptions as embeddings. It is surprising 
that models relying on unaligned natural language embeddings out-
perform those leveraging transcriptome-informed embeddings. T o 
investigate the underlying source of performance gain, we conducted 
an ablation study designed to isolate the contribution of different 
embedding types. Specifically, we replaced the original embeddings 
in GenePert and scouter with five alternative embeddings: Gene -
Compass29, geneformer9, scBERT8, scELMo28 and scGPT7, while keeping 
all other model parameters and training procedures identical. Among 
these, scELMo and ChatGPT-based embeddings are derived from  
large language models (LLMs) trained on biomedical corpora, whereas 
GeneCompass, geneformer, scBERT and scGPT are derived from  
foundation models trained on large-scale single-cell transcriptomic 
datasets. We evaluated model performance using MSE and PCC-delta, 
as these metrics more accurately reflect model performance and  
are more computationally efficient 50. As shown, across nine single-  
gene perturbation datasets, GenePert and scouter consistently 
achieved top performance when using embeddings from scELMo and 
ChatGPT (Supplementary Fig. 45). In contrast, performance dropped 
markedly when transcriptome-informed embeddings (for example, 
from scGPT and geneformer) were used. Notably, GenePert uses  
a linear regression model, and scouter uses a simple multilayer per -
ceptron—yet both outperform more complex models such as GEARS 
and biolord under certain settings. This suggests that the performance 
advantage likely stems from the rich biological priors embedded

across three perturbation categories: combo-seen2, combo-seen1 
and combo-seen0 (Fig. 4d). These categories correspond to scenar -

in the training set, respectively, which increase the difficulty of  
achieving high accuracy 5,52. linearModel and scouter achieved the 
highest overall accuracy across all categories (Fig. 4d). Accurately 
predicting combined-perturbation effects fundamentally relies on 
correctly learning the interactions between genes. Therefore, we used 
the Norman dataset to assess the ability of current models to predict 
gene interactions
40. In this dataset, gene interactions are categorized 
into seven types, including additive, suppressor, synergy and other 
types (Supplementary Note 10). The additive-based linearModel and 
trainMean achieved the best performance on the additive interaction 
type, which is in line with expectations (Fig. 4e). Additionally, CPA 
performed best on the potentiation, epistasis and redundant gene 
interaction types, which may indicate that deep learning models have 
certain advantages in predicting specific types of gene interactions 
(Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 25).
Among the combo-seen2, combo-seen1 and combo-seen0  
perturbation types, predicting the perturbation effects for combo-  
seen0 was the most challenging and placed greater demands on the 
model’s generalization ability, as both perturbations are unseen. 
In all four combined-perturbation datasets, the performance of all 
tools is the worst for combo-seen0 prediction (Fig. 4g). Notably, in 
the Schmidt dataset38, scGPT substantially outperformed the baseline 
model in terms of the MSE and PCC-delta metrics, especially for the 
combo-seen0 perturbation type (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 26). 
This may further demonstrate the superior generalizability of the 
foundation model in certain test scenarios.
We also assessed the scalability of algorithms and the robustness 
of the algorithms to technical noise and data sparsity using simulated 
data (Methods). The procedure and evaluation metrics were similar  
to those in the cellular context generalization scenario. In terms of  
scalability, none of the algorithms encountered out-of-memory  
issues, and their runtimes were within acceptable limits, except for 
scFoundation (Fig. 4i). All the algorithms’ performance was affected 
by data noise and sparsity, with greater sensitivity to sparsity (Fig. 4a,j). 
Owing to the long runtime of scFoundation, we did not evaluate 
its robustness.
Chemical perturbation setting.  For the three single-perturbation 
datasets, chemCPA achieved the best performance on the 
population-average metrics, outperforming the baseline model by 
a substantial margin (Fig. 5a). Unlike other deep learning models, 
chemCPA performed impressively even on smaller datasets, likely 
because of its effective use of prior knowledge during pretraining on 
bulk RNA-seq datasets 53, which enriched its understanding of gene 
expression patterns. In the combined-perturbation dataset, baseReg 
outperformed CPA on all six metrics, highlighting the current gap in 
methods for chemical perturbation prediction, particularly in combi-
natorial contexts (Fig. 5b). Therefore, developing more effective tools 
for such scenarios remains an urgent priority.
Next, we assessed the robustness and scalability of the evalu -
ated algorithms (Methods). The procedure and evaluation metrics 
are similar to those in the cellular context generalization scenario. 
In terms of scalability (Fig. 5c ), none of the algorithms encountered 
out-of-memory issues, and their runtimes were within acceptable 
limits. Regarding the impact of sparsity, PRnet and chemCPA were the 
most stable. Regarding the impact of noise, PRnet and cycleCDR were 
the most stable (Fig. 5a,d).
In summary, the following insights were obtained from the 
benchmark analysis of the perturbation generalization scenario:  
(1) T o predict genetic single-perturbation effects, GenePert is an 
optimal choic
e when the training dataset is small. However, when 
the fine-tuning dataset is sufficient, deep learning and foundation 
models such as CPA and scGPT are preferable. (2) In predicting genetic 
combined-perturbation effects, linearModel and scouter perform the 
best. (3) In chemical single-perturbation effect prediction, chemCPA  
is the preferred choice. (4) At present, methods for predicting  
chemical combined-perturbation effects are limited, and the baseReg 
baseline model achieves the highest accuracy.

Discussion
In this study, we provide a comprehensive benchmark of single-cell 
perturbation prediction methods across two key scenarios: cellular 
context generalization and perturbation generalization. We assessed 
27 methods with a total of 29 datasets using 6 evaluation metrics—
MSE, PCC-delta, E-distance, Wasserstein distance, KL-divergence and 
Common-DEGs—with a focus on evaluating the methods’ generaliz -
ability. Simulated data were also used to assess the impact of noise 
and sparsity on performance. Our findings revealed that no single 
method performs well across all datasets, highlighting the need for 
user-specific guidance in selecting methods on the basis of dataset 
characteristics. These results are as follows: (1) In the cellular context 
generalization scenario, trVAE, CellOT and inVAE demonstrated the 
best overall performance on single-condition datasets. For the accurate 
prediction of the top most differentially expressed genes, scPRAM is the 
optimal choice. Additionally, when the dataset includes perturbation 
dosage information, scVIDR is the best method. (2) In the perturbation 
generalization scenario, for predicting genetic single-perturbation 
effects, GenePert is the optimal choice when the training dataset is 
small. When the fine-tuning dataset is sufficiently large, CPA and scGPT 
are preferable. For predicting genetic combined-perturbation effects, 
the linearModel and scouter method perform the best. In chemical 
single-perturbation effect prediction, chemCPA is the preferred choice, 
whereas the baseReg baseline model achieves the highest accuracy in 
chemical combined-perturbation effect prediction (Fig. 5g).
For designing future algorithms, the following limitations of exist-
ing methods and insights should be considered: (1) In the cellular 
context generalization scenario, most existing tools do not incorporate 
time-point/dosage covariates, and those that do are still far from suf-
ficiently accurate. Given the increasing availability of multi-condition 
data in the single-cell perturbation field, there is a pressing need to 
develop high-performing models tailored for such data. (2) In both 
test scenarios, despite accounting for intra-heterogeneity, the models 
exhibited weaker performance on population-distribution metrics 
compared to population-average metrics. Typically, deep learning 
models in the perturbation effect prediction field are optimized using 
loss functions that focus more on population-average metrics. This may 
account for the strong performance of these methods on such metrics 
and their poorer results regarding population-distribution metrics. 
However, understanding the heterogeneity within cellular subpopula-
tions in response to perturbations is vital for revealing gene functions 
and regulatory networks. T o increase prediction accuracy, future mod-
els for perturbation effect prediction may consider incorporating both 
population-average and population-distribution metrics into their 
loss function design; (3) One limitation of current methods emerges 
in the perturbation generalization setting, where advanced models 
tend to underperform relative to simpler approaches, particularly on 
small-scale datasets. This finding suggests that complex models may 
struggle to generalize effectively in low-data regimes. Enhancing the 
robustness and generalizability of advanced models under data-limited 
conditions represents an important direction for future research.  
(4) All current methods likely exhibited poor generalizability, espe -
cially when predicting perturbation effects in novel cellular contexts. 
T o alleviate this, we propose incorporating prior knowledge through 
cell-line embeddings, which can improve model generalizability (Sup-
plementary Notes 12 and 13 and Supplementary Figs. 27, 28 and 38). 
As a proof-of-concept study, we present an initial attempt to inte -
grate such prior knowledge and formulate an applicable framework  
with validations. We encourage researchers to explore and develop 
alternative approaches on the basis of their own deeper understanding 
and hope that our approach will inspire future work and drive innova-
tion in this area.
There are several limitations of our current research. (1) In 
certain test scenarios, the available datasets are currently insuffi -
cient, which may lead to bias in the conclusions drawn; (2) Our pro -
posed cellular context embedding strategy is currently applicable 
only to cell-line-derived contexts and cannot be directly applied to 
patient-derived contexts (Supplementary Note 12); (3) While the pro-
posed cellular context embedding strategy represents a conceptu -
ally promising direction, its current performance demonstrates only 
limited improvements—mainly in the PCC-delta and Common-DEGs 
metrics—and does not consistently surpass baseline methods across 
evaluation metrics on all the evaluated datasets. As a preliminary 
attempt, this method remains an early exploration, and we acknowl-
edge that further methodological refinements will be necessary to 
improve its performance and enhance its practical applicability;  
(4) Our current work does not address the challenge of poor perfor -
mance on population-distribution metrics, which reflect a model’s 
ability to capture intracellular heterogeneity. Addressing this limitation 
remains an important direction for future research; (5) One limitation 
of our current robustness evaluation framework is that it primarily 
focuses on the effects of technical artifacts, such as noise and sparsity. 
While this allows us to systematically assess the sensitivity of predic-
tive models to common experimental distortions, it does not capture 
biological variability in perturbation responses across different cellular 
contexts or genetic backgrounds. Evaluating robustness to biological 
variation with simulator tools such as scDesign3 (ref. 54 ) remains an 
important and complementary direction for future work.
Looking ahead, several important directions could further 
advance the field of single-cell perturbation outcome prediction.  
(1) There is an urgent need to establish more diverse and compre -
hensive single-cell perturbation atlases. Similarly to how large-scale 
single-cell atlases have accelerated algorithmic development in the 
broader single-cell field, systematically building perturbation-focused 
cell atlases could catalyze progress in perturbation effect prediction. 
Notably, Rood et al. have proposed the concept of a ‘perturbation 
cell atlas’ , highlighting the value of such systematic efforts for the 
community55. Large-scale resources such as Tahoe-100M and X-Atlas 
have begun to fill this gap by providing comprehensive perturbation 
datasets, which lay a strong foundation for future benchmarking

improve the robustness and interpretability of perturbation prediction 
models; (3) Despite promising advances, the field of chemical pertur-

effects, remains underdeveloped. More dedicated research is needed to 
develop specialized algorithms capable of accurately modeling chemi-

Methods
Settings of benchmark methods
In this study, we selected a range of advanced modeling approaches 
to systematically compare their ability to predict single-cell perturba-
tion effects. The models include diverse theoretical frameworks and 
algorithmic structures, ranging from linear models to foundation 
models, and other deep learning-based approaches. In this study, we 
evaluated the methods in two key scenarios: cellular context gener -
alization and perturbation generalization. In the cellular context gen-
eralization scenario, we selected 14 methods, including 10 published 
methods, namely, biolord16, CellOT14, inVAE17, scDisInFact18, scGen4, 
scPRAM15, scPreGAN19, SCREEN20, scVIDR21 and trVAE22 and 4 baseline 
models. In the perturbation generalization scenario, we examined 18 
methods: AttentionPert 23, biolord16, CPA24, GEARS5, GenePert25, lin-
earModel12, scFoundation6, scGPT7, chemCPA26, scouter27, scELMo28, 
GeneCompass29, PRnet30, cycleCDR31 and 4 baseline models. For each 
method, the parameters were set according to official guidelines 
and tailored to align with our benchmarking datasets. Please refer 
to Supplementary Note 1 for detailed descriptions and settings of all 
benchmark methods.
Datasets used in our benchmark
For the cellular generalization scenario, we collected 12 datasets. These 
datasets include various cell types, species nd individuals, making 
them suitable for studying predictions at multiple cellular levels. The 
datasets were all downloaded from public repositories and the NCBI 
Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Specifically, the kangCrossCell, 
kangCrossPatient, and Haber datasets use preprocessed data from Lot-
follahi et al., which can be downloaded directly from Google Drive via 
https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1n1SLbXha4OH7j7zZ0zZAxrj_-
2kczgl8/. kangCrossCell and kangCrossPatient consist of data from 
human peripheral blood mononuclear cells from eight donors stimu-
lated with interferon-beta and the corresponding control groups 33. 
The original data can be obtained from the GEO under the identifier 
GSE96583. The Haber dataset includes data of intestinal epithelial 
cells infected with bacterial and helminth infections, and the original 
dataset is available from the GEO under the identifier GSE92332 (ref. 
37). The Parekh, CrossPatient and sciPlex3 datasets were obtained 
from the PerturBase database 46 (http://www.perturbase.cn/). The 
Parekh perturbation dataset contains overexpression data for eight 
transcription factors across three cell lines58. The CrossPatient dataset 
includes single-cell perturbation data for six patients in response to 
two chemicals, Panobinostat and Etoposide 35. The sciPlex3 dataset 
comprises measurements from three human cancer cell lines (A549, 
MCF7 and K562) subjected to perturbations with 188 chemicals at 
four different dosages1. As suggested in a previous study, only data 
for the nine most effective chemicals were used for testing 16. The  
KaggleCrossPatient and KaggleCrossCell datasets were derived from 
the Open Problems competition. The organizers provided peripheral 
blood mononuclear cell data from three donors, including data from 
five cell types exposed to 144 chemicals. These datasets can be down-
loaded from the Kaggle competition webpage via https://www.kaggle.
com/competitions/open-problems-single-cell-perturbations/data/. 
The McFarland 32 dataset contains measurements from six human 
cancer cell lines exposed to perturbations with 11 chemicals and  
was downloaded from the scPerturb database 13 (version 1.3), which 
is available at Zenodo via https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10044268  
(ref. 59). The CrossSpecies 34 dataset contains expression data from  
four species (mouse, rat, rabbit and pig). The processed dataset is  
available at https://github.com/theislab/scgen-reproducibility/
blob/master/code/DataDownloader.py/. The Afriat perturbation 
dataset60 was downloaded from the biolord GitHub tutorial site via 
https://biolord.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorials/biolord_pipe -
line.html. The detailed information of these datasets is given in 
Supplementary Table 3.
For the perturbation generalization scenario, we curated 17

tions and 4 focus on combined perturbations, and 4 chemical per-

combined-perturbation dataset. The numbers of perturbations in these

Data preprocessing
All single-cell perturbation datasets used in our study were subjected 
to the following quality-control procedures. (1) Cell filtering. Cells 
were excluded if they expressed fewer than 200 genes, or exhibited a 
high mitochondrial gene fraction exceeding 10%. Furthermore, in the 
context of benchmarking models for the perturbation generalization 
scenario, cells subjected to perturbations in Schmidt dataset by at 
most two genes were retained, given the predominance of two-gene 
combinatorial perturbation data in the currently available datasets, 
resulting in the exclusion of 5,942 cells. We excluded any perturba -
tions, other than the control, which affected fewer than 30 cells from 
our analysis. Additionally, to address the issue of computational effi-
ciency, the perturbations with a large number of cells need further to 
be processed. Specifically, for perturbations affecting more than 2,000 
cells, we used a random selection method to include only 2,000 cells 
in our downstream analysis. This data preprocessing strategy allows 
us to balance computational resources and time without substantially 
compromising model accuracy (Supplementary Note 15). (2) Gene 
filtering. Genes that were expressed in fewer than three cells were 
removed. In the context of benchmarking models for perturbation 
generalization scenario, genes that did not have embeddings from 
scGPT and scFoundation were excluded for each dataset. T o balance the 
need for managing computational complexity and preserving maximal 
information, we constrained each dataset to the 5,000 genes exhibit-
ing the highest variability. This decision aligns with the preprocessing 
protocol established by the majority methods. During the selection 
of highly variable genes, we first split the entire dataset into training 
and testing sets. The highly variable genes are selected using only the 
training set, and these selected genes are then used to evaluate model 
performance on the testing set. This procedure helps to minimize 
the risk of information leakage. Additionally, we ensured the inclu -
sion of any genes that were specifically targeted by perturbations but 
not already represented among these highly variable genes, thereby 
maintaining the integrity of our dataset for analyses involving these 
perturbed genes. (3) Data normalization. We used the global scaling 
normalization technique provided by Scanpy, which standardizes

sequencing depth.
Evaluation metrics
T o ensure a fair, interpretable and representative evaluation of pertur-
bation prediction models, we conducted an extensive literature survey 
covering 28 existing tools and benchmarking papers in the related 
field (Supplementary Table 1). From these sources, we identified 19 
evaluation metrics, including MSE, PCC-delta, root mean squared 
error (RMSE), maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), Euclidean distance, 
E-distance, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test statistic, mean absolute error, 
two-sided t-test statistic, cosine similarity, PCC, coefficient of determi-
nation, classifier control probability, Kendall tau distance, Spearman 
correlation coefficient, Wasserstein distance, KL-divergence, classifier 
class projection and Common-DEGs.
Based on this pool of metrics, we applied four guiding princi -
ples to select a subset of representative and informative metrics for 
our study: (1) Accuracy. We prioritized metrics that have been dem -
onstrated to reliably assess model performance in prior studies. In 
particular, we referred to a comprehensive benchmark by Jing et al., 
which systematically evaluated and ranked the performance of various 
metrics in the context of single-cell perturbation prediction; (2) Com-
prehensiveness. We aimed to include metrics that evaluate different 
facets of model performance. Specifically, we distinguished between 
population-average metrics (which assess the prediction of average 
expression profiles) and population-distribution metrics (which assess 
how well a model predicts the full distribution of single-cell profiles); (3) 
Widespread usage. Among a group of metrics, we favored those most 
widely adopted in the literature to improve comparability with exist-
ing methods and minimize subjective bias in metric selection. Among 
those 19 metrics, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, two-sided t-test, classifier 
control probability, classifier class projection and Kendall tau distance 
were only mentioned in the benchmark study by Jing et al. Moreover, 
their performance was generally inferior to that of MSE. Including such 
metrics, which are less sensitive or poorly correlated with prediction 
quality, could introduce noise or misleading conclusions into model 
comparison. Therefore, these metrics were excluded from further 
consideration. (4) Non-redundancy. T o avoid overrepresentation of 
similar performance aspects, we carefully excluded highly correlated 
or mathematically equivalent metrics. For example, RMSE is linearly 
related to MSE, and linear MMD and Euclidean distance are functionally 
equivalent to MSE after proper scaling. T o further visualize the cor-
relations between evaluation metrics, we selected two representative 
datasets from each of the two key scenarios—cellular context generali-
zation and perturbation generalization—and computed pairwise cor-
relations among the 13 commonly used metrics. The results revealed 
that many of these metrics are highly correlated, which aligns with 
their underlying mathematical formulations (Extended Data Fig. 1). 
T o reduce redundancy and simplify interpretation, we retained only 
the most representative metric from each highly correlated group, 
prioritizing those with the highest accuracy or clearest interpretability. 
This ensures a more concise and nonoverlapping set of metrics for 
model evaluation.
Following these principles, we selected three population-average 
metrics and three population-distribution metrics. The population-  
average metrics assess the model’s ability to predict average values, 
whereas the population-distribution metrics require the model to 
accurately predict the distribution of single-cell expression profiles.

Ranking the methods
We used a straightforward ranking-based strategy to evaluate the 
relative performance of the selected methods across the six metrics. 
Specifically, within each dataset, the methods were ranked on the basis 
of their raw outputs for each metric. The overall ranking of a method in 
a given dataset was then calculated by averaging its ranks across the six 
metrics. T o obtain a final ranking across all the datasets, we averaged 
the overall rankings from each dataset. This ranking-based approach 
addresses potential inconsistencies in value ranges between different 
metrics, ensuring a fair comparison.

have ranges between 0 and 1, while MSE, E-distance, Wasserstein

Calculation of the similarity between cellular contexts in the 
cellular context generalization scenario
In the cellular context generalization scenario, we calculated the simi-
larity between the test context and each context in the training dataset 
via the E-distance. Specifically, we reduced the unperturbed control 
group in each context to 5,000 dimensions using principal component 
analysis and then measured the distance between pairs of contexts 
using the E-distance13 function from the Pertpy Python package62. The 
minimum value was used to assess the degree of similarity between 
the test context and the training contexts. A larger value indicates a 
lower similarity.
Evaluation of the strength of a perturbation in the 
perturbation generalization scenario
For each perturbation, we calculated the E-distance between the unper-
turbed control group and the perturbed group. Specifically, both 
groups were reduced to 5,000 dimensions using principal component 
analysis, and the E-distance function from the Pertpy Python package 
was used to quantify the distance between them62. The distance value 
was used to represent the strength of a perturbation and a larger value 
indicates a stronger effect.
The generation of simulated data with different level of noise 
and sparsity
We used the script from Yuan et al. to generate simulated data, which 
allowed us to evaluate how noise and sparsity impact the performance 
of the methods. In their original work, Yuan et al. introduced varying 
levels of noise and sparsity into spatially resolved transcriptomics data 
to systematically assess the robustness of spatial clustering methods. 
Following a similar rationale, we applied controlled varying levels of 
noise and sparsity into real datasets, allowing us to simulate realistic 
experimental challenges and evaluate model performance under vary-
ing levels of technical noise and dropout. The process of generating the 
simulated data can be broken down into two parts: adding noise and 
introducing sparsity: (1) Adding noise. T o introduce noise into the gene 
expression data, we applied Poisson noise at varying levels (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 
0.7 and 0.9). The Poisson noise was generated by sampling from a Pois-
son distribution where the rate parameter (λ) for each gene expression 
value was determined by multiplying the original expression values by 
the specified noise level. For example, at a noise level of 0.1, the noise 
added to each gene expression value was proportional to 10% of the 
original expression value. The generated Poisson noise was then added 
to the original gene expression matrix, producing a noisy version of 
the dataset. This approach allows us to simulate gene expression data 
with varying levels of noise intensity, which is useful for assessing the 
impact of different noise levels on model performance; (2) Introducing 
sparsity. For sparsity, we randomly removed gene expression values 
at different rates (0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9). For instance, when the 
removal rate is set to 0.9, each gene expression value has a 90% prob-
ability of being set to zero, making the data highly sparse. Conversely, 
a removal rate of 0 means that no values are removed, and the gene 
expression matrix remains unchanged. This process simulates various 
levels of sparsity in the data and allows us to evaluate how different 
sparsity levels affect the performance of the model. The detailed infor-
mation of these simulated datasets is given in Supplementary Table 4.
Computational resources
Most of the benchmark tasks were conducted on an Ubuntu18 system 
with a 96-core CPU, with 1,000 GB of RAM and four NVIDIA 3090 24 GB 
GPUs. The scFoundation model has high GPU memory requirements, so 
we ran it on a server with two NVIDIA A6000 48 GB GPUs. Both servers 
used the Ubuntu 18.04 operating system.
