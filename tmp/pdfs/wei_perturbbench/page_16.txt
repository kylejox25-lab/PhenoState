Evaluation metrics
T o ensure a fair, interpretable and representative evaluation of pertur-
bation prediction models, we conducted an extensive literature survey 
covering 28 existing tools and benchmarking papers in the related 
field (Supplementary Table 1). From these sources, we identified 19 
evaluation metrics, including MSE, PCC-delta, root mean squared 
error (RMSE), maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), Euclidean distance, 
E-distance, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test statistic, mean absolute error, 
two-sided t-test statistic, cosine similarity, PCC, coefficient of determi-
nation, classifier control probability, Kendall tau distance, Spearman 
correlation coefficient, Wasserstein distance, KL-divergence, classifier 
class projection and Common-DEGs.
Based on this pool of metrics, we applied four guiding princi -
ples to select a subset of representative and informative metrics for 
our study: (1) Accuracy. We prioritized metrics that have been dem -
onstrated to reliably assess model performance in prior studies. In 
particular, we referred to a comprehensive benchmark by Jing et al., 
which systematically evaluated and ranked the performance of various 
metrics in the context of single-cell perturbation prediction; (2) Com-
prehensiveness. We aimed to include metrics that evaluate different 
facets of model performance. Specifically, we distinguished between 
population-average metrics (which assess the prediction of average 
expression profiles) and population-distribution metrics (which assess 
how well a model predicts the full distribution of single-cell profiles); (3) 
Widespread usage. Among a group of metrics, we favored those most 
widely adopted in the literature to improve comparability with exist-
ing methods and minimize subjective bias in metric selection. Among 
those 19 metrics, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, two-sided t-test, classifier 
control probability, classifier class projection and Kendall tau distance 
were only mentioned in the benchmark study by Jing et al. Moreover, 
their performance was generally inferior to that of MSE. Including such 
metrics, which are less sensitive or poorly correlated with prediction 
quality, could introduce noise or misleading conclusions into model 
comparison. Therefore, these metrics were excluded from further 
consideration. (4) Non-redundancy. T o avoid overrepresentation of 
similar performance aspects, we carefully excluded highly correlated 
or mathematically equivalent metrics. For example, RMSE is linearly 
related to MSE, and linear MMD and Euclidean distance are functionally 
equivalent to MSE after proper scaling. T o further visualize the cor-
relations between evaluation metrics, we selected two representative 
datasets from each of the two key scenarios—cellular context generali-
zation and perturbation generalization—and computed pairwise cor-
relations among the 13 commonly used metrics. The results revealed 
that many of these metrics are highly correlated, which aligns with 
their underlying mathematical formulations (Extended Data Fig. 1). 
T o reduce redundancy and simplify interpretation, we retained only 
the most representative metric from each highly correlated group, 
prioritizing those with the highest accuracy or clearest interpretability. 
This ensures a more concise and nonoverlapping set of metrics for 
model evaluation.
Following these principles, we selected three population-average 
metrics and three population-distribution metrics. The population-  
average metrics assess the model’s ability to predict average values, 
whereas the population-distribution metrics require the model to 
accurately predict the distribution of single-cell expression profiles.
